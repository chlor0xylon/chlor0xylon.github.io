ImageNet-1K is a substantial dataset of deep computer vision. It paved the way for visual recognition progress in the early 2010s. This post is about setting up a small experiment to test the outputs of a more recent SOTA vision model on the images of that dataset, and take advantage of the fact that there is a known structure to the labels to visualize the results.


## The WordNet hierarchy

So, ImageNet-1K [@imagenet1k2012] contains 1.4 million images split into 1000 categories. These categories also happen to belong to a much larger set organized into a hierarchy called [WordNet](https://wordnet.princeton.edu/), which is a 2005 project compiling a lexical database modelling the semantic relationships between 82000+ English words, expressions, and textual concepts [@imagenet2009].

The 1000 ImageNet classes are diverse but a lot of them are in fact variants or specialized versions of broader common species, objects, or concepts. A full list can be found [here](https://deeplearning.cms.waikato.ac.nz/user-guide/class-maps/IMAGENET/). For example, there are dozens of different dog species. Any two entities of the WordNet hierarchy related by a subset-ordering relation are linked in the graph by a directed edge going from a concept to its parent. Thus, expectedly, the whole WordNet takes the form of a **direct, acyclic graph** (DAG) whose single sink node is named "entity", and is comprised of 74401 nodes and 75850 edges.

Looking at partial graph representations of the WordNet graph induced by the nodes that are present in ImageNet classes reveals a much smaller underlying structure comprised of 1860 nodes and 1937 remaining edges. To visualize it, I assembled the DAG from the node and edge text dumps available on seemingly long-forgotten GitHub repositories. In the coming figures, I inverted arrows to point to descendents instead of ancestors in order to convey the more intuitive idea "is a subset of" as opposed to "is a superset of". Moreover I pruned nodes that did not have any leaves that are part of ImageNet's one thousand classes. There is no point rendering the whole subgraph in this post, as the figure would not fit anyway, but producing a whole PDF of it is possible. <a data-figure-ref="fig-entity-to-dog"></a> and <a data-figure-ref="fig-root-depth-4"></a> show such visual excerpts of the graph: all paths leading to metaclass "dog" on one hand and all reachable metaclass nodes from the root within 4 steps (final ImageNet class leaves were stripped).

## Condensing the WordNet structure onto ImageNet categories

Graph-based data enable all kings of interesting analysis potentials. Graphs condense information in a discrete structure different from continuous spaces, like regular real vectors. But graph data can incidentally be converted to vectors via [spectral analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_graph_theory), to produce equivalent embeddings that can, in turn, be projected to lower a dimensionality space and be shown in usual dot plots, for example. Or, such embeddings could also be converted to visual cues like color attributes on the noeds (but not necessarily a graph coloring). This is what I will be doing below.


### Designing a comparison kernel for leaf nodes
In order to proceed with that idea, I first contracted transitory nodes (nodes with exactly one ancestor and one decendant) from the whole WordNet DAG. Although these may encode existing category subdivisions, they actually add noise to the graph structure for computations like distance between nodes and spectral analysis without serving a true branching purpose. So, I contracted them. That leaves us with only 1416 nodes (416 of which are intermediary metaclasses) and 1489 edges.

Secondly, since we're more interested in the final 1000 WordNet categories that are ImageNet classes--in order to perform downstream computer vision-based analysis with that graph prior at hand--, embeddings of the remaining intermediary metaclasses are useless to compute.


So, how could we produce embeddings for the leaf nodes of a DAG alone? Well, I came up with the idea of treating the DAG as an elaborate proxy for a simpler similarity matrix. In other words, it is about deriving a comparison metric kernel based on the DAG to compute an equivalent complete weighted graph between leaves. The underlying hypothesis is that the more similar class nodes are, the closer they are clustered together in the hierarchical graph. The same goes for leaves. So I wrote a function that calls `networkx.single_source_shortest_path_length` on every leaf and derives the lowest-common-ancestor-based distance between any two given leaves $u,v$ of the DAG. Then I derived the similarity metric by stating:

$$
\text{Similarity}(u, v) = \frac{1}{1 + \text{Distance}(u, v)}
$$

where $\text{Distance}(u, v)$ is effectively the sum of distances from the LCA to each of the two leaves: $d_{\text{LCA} \rightarrow u} + d_{\text{LCA} \rightarrow v}$.

The fully formed matrix of similarities for every pair of leaves emulates the representation of a complete graph with weighted edges and nodes looping onto themselves (the original DAG is fully connected, so every pair of leaves has a LCA).
The resulting matrix of similarity $S \in \mathbb{R}^{1000 \times 1000}$ is symmetric with positive entries and can be reused at will for computing embeddings.


### Computing spectral embeddings
Spectral embeddings, usually leverage a normalized version of the [Laplacian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian_matrix) $L=D-A$ of the graph where $D$ is the diagonal matrix of degrees of nodes, and $A$ is the adjacency matrix of the graph. As a side note about numerical computations, extracting eigenelements of a 1000 by 1000 matrix is definitely getting into the "not trivial anymore" territory of numerical linear algebra (because of numerical stability), but it remains completely tractable with modern libraries, especially as we don't need the whole spectrum. At first, I set out to compute eigenvectors corresponding to the largest eigenvalues using Scipy's `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh` on the manually preprocessed Laplacian but I ended up using SkLearn's `sklearn.manifold.SpectralEmbedding` with the `affinity="precomputed"` flag as an easier API reference.

So I computed embeddings in 3D for the artificial subgraph made up from the affinity metric on ImageNet's 1000 classes, which amounts to creating three attribution metrics and yielding three numbers for every node, which can be then normalized and converted to RGB colors.

### Results

The resulting colormaps are interesting: with three spectral embedding dimensions and thus three color components, a few predictable groups appear. The aforementioned prominent meta-classes end up with almost the same color. For example, canines appear in bright yellow and orange shades. Fruits and vegetables get brown. Inert objects range across a wide blue and green palette whereas a lot of animals receive in pink and fushia colors. Refer to  <a data-figure-ref="fig-complete-graph-with-colormap"></a>.

Another remarkable result of the spectral embeddings based on the previous speculated similarity metric is that they are able to successfully summarize coarser, unexpected aspects already contained in the WordNet. With one spectral embedding dimension instead of three,  shown here with a red-blue colormap, a clear boundary materializes and seems to consistently separate living entities from inert entities, with mainly food items lying on ambiguous neutral ground (<a data-figure-ref="fig-bicolor-hierarchy"></a>). This, in my opinion, is surprising because the top-level splits from the original DAG (<a data-figure-ref="fig-root-depth-4"></a>) do not explicitly encode or suggest the "alive" characteristic until depth 4 with "organism, being", and yet that property resurfaces from the finer, deeper subdivisions of the DAG as a main spectral direction.

## The DINOv3 hierarchy

In contrast with the previously presented discrete hierarchy built upon textual elements, the DINOv3 vision model family of neural networks is trained on the LVD-1689M dataset [@simeoni2025dinov3] to produce continuous latent representations of images. The models in that family were trained to produce generic image embeddings in dense high-dimensional spaces whose dimensions range from 384 to 4096 dimensions depending on the backbone and model size. Those embeddings can naturally be used for downstream tasks, although I'm not interested in doing that. Rather, I'm interested in testing whether or not these raw embeddings independently reproduce the WordNet hierarchy. 


### Assessing the match
The fact that the categories of the ImageNet dataset are part of a larger knowledge graph with known metaclass dependencies makes it particularly interesting to use as a prior for the image data. **So, it stands as an interesting question to me whether the ImageNet class hierarchy we unveiled earlier translates to the unsupervised embeddings of that dataset through a completely independent model.** It is about testing whether language hiararchies are consistent with visual hierarchies obtained by means of an unsupervised, emergent learning strategy[^note-emergence].


[^note-emergence]: In the first DINO paper, the authors ecplicitly described that their learning student-teacher networks featured emergent properties by learning embeddings with no labels at all, driven only by crops and partial views.

#### With a planar dimensionality reduction technique

To test the above hypothesis, I ran a 2D UMAP dimensionality reduction on the CLS tokens of 250000 unaltered random ImageNet samples (about a fifth of the total dataset size) through DINOv3 ConvNeXt Tiny variant (see <a data-figure-ref="fig-umap-imagenet"></a>), and I colored the points depending in their label with the RGB colormap derived from the aforementioned similarity spectral embedding.

The conclusion is already clear: at least on this very model, at least to some extent, the CLS token embeddings _does_ reflect the language-built prior structure provided by WordNet. Pretty neat, isn't it?

## Conclusion
This was a fun experiment bridging together research data and models that are more than 20 years apart. I had heard about the Platonic Representation Hypothesis [@huh2024platonicrepresentationhypothesis] suggesting that all deep learning models converge to representations that share a common structure. This small-scale experiment supports a claim that may not be very groundbreaking, but it confirms that modern vision models have captured representations that roughly follow the same preconceptions as humans put on language, according to plausible metrics. I might want to try and extend the testing with more elaborate language embeddings like Word2Vec [@word2vec] or even LLMs one day.
