This is the second part of a series on reproducing recent advancements in computer vision interpretability. In the [first part](/posts/dinov3-prototypes-part-1/), I introduced the history of feature visualization. Here, I attempt to reproduce a dictionary learning approach on the DINOv3 model.

## Dictionary learning: an introduction

Before visualizing concepts as images, we need to identify semantically representative directions in the model's latent space to gain better understanding of how visuals are encoded. This can be achieved thanks to a prominent method in ML interpretability research called _dictionary learning_.

Broadly speaking, dictionary learning seeks to discover a set of building blocks to express complex data as a sparse combination of these patterns--sometimes referred to as _atoms_--that together form the _dictionary_. An efficient dictionary should provide compact, meaningful representations that are useful for interpretability purposes, denoising, or feature extraction for example. Unlike PCA or SVD, dictionary learning approaches usually attempt to make the decomposition _sparse_. In that sense, it is close in spirit to data compression. Because of that, dictionary learning is also known as _sparse coding_.

:::note
Data compression works by seeking a representation basis to encode the data in the most compact way possible. For example, the JPEG standard breaks down each $8 \times 8$ block of pixels into a sum of cosine-based waveforms. It turns out that real-world image signals are usually better explained by smooth variations resembling a sparse combination of cosine waves, and this better prior it allows a lower-rank representation to match human perceptual vision with fewer resources. Sparsity is a desirable property when doing coding, either for storage saving purposes, or for the sake of interpretability.
:::


### Formal definition
I will rely on @holistic2023fel, a paper that reviews the concept extraction variants attempts in the literature and compares them all and identifies their commonalities. One of the latest methods presented was coined CRAFT, standing for _Concept Recursive Activation FacTorization for Explainability_ and was taken from the preceding paper @craft2023fel, which adds non-negativity constraint to the learned priors. In the paper, it is claimed indeed that the positivity of the decomposition 
- "encourages sparsity"
- "allows missing data to be imputed"
- "enhances the interpretability of the decomposition"

:::note
The first claim is obvious because negative entries will force the concept vectors to keep many null entries to be able to encode zero entries, the closest approximation of any negative value projected onto the half-line $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$. Regarding the second claim, the authors develop an attribution method for visualizing the concept activations in images and rely on preceding research to support their statement, but here I'm only interested in the part about dictionary learning, and I don't know or care about it for now. Regarding the third one, my understanding of the approach is that only positive activations are considered meaningful because they represent strongly-typed features, whereas negative activations do not carry significant information similar to how negative logit values would compress into irrelevancy and produce near-zero probabilities that are overshadowed by positive logits.
:::

The setup goes like this: let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be a set of $n$ captured activations in dimension $p$ from the latents from a vision model taken from a provided collection of images. Let $r$ be a positive integer, denoted as the rank of the decomposition. The positive dictionary learning approach consists OF solving the following problem:

$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{U}\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r}, \, \mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{p\times r}} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{UW}^\top \|_F^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{W}, \mathbf{U} \ge 0
$$

where $\mathbf{W}$ is the _dictionary of concepts_ onto which the dataset is projected and $\mathbf{U}$ is the dataset decomposition, i.e. the assignments of each data point to each of the $r$ concepts. For dictionary learning to be meaningful, $r$ and $n$ must be chosen such that $r < n$.

Note that $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{W}$ are required to belong to the positive orthant of matrices with **non-negative entries**. That constraint is a deliberate modeling choice. Note that the solution space is convex, but the search is made arduous because positivity prevents the use of off-the-shelf linear algebra results. This formulated problem  is known as the **Non-Negative Least Squares problem** (NNLS), and I first encountered during my Master's Thesis in the context of beamforming in transmission-related problems.


### Naïve solving procedure
Solving the above mathematical problem can be approached in at least a few different manners. In Fel's PhD thesis [@fel2025sparksexplainabilityrecentadvancements], section C.1.3 of the manuscript along the implementation details of CRAFT, they explain that they used a method called alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM for short), which I also encountered during my Master's Thesis, because it arises a lot in the literature about applied mathematical optimization problems with cumbersome constraints such as non-negativity. There is no point outlining ADMM in this post, as it is better explained in numerous other papers, articles and posts freely available, and I won't use it anyway.


### Toward a more holistic approach
@holistic2023fel published an implementation of the CRAFT method resulting in an interactive website called the [Lens Project](https://serre-lab.github.io/Lens/) that allows to navigate and visualize them. They only retain a few major concepts per class which sums to quite a large pool, but they did not actually compute them across the whole dataset at once, as far as I understand. Rather, the dictionary is computed on a per-class basis and is low-rank from the start to only retain a handful of concepts per class (they take 10). In a later stage, their attribution method yields the contribution scores to each class.


On one hand, this significantly lowers the numerical complexity requirements since it cuts by a factor of 1000 the effective dataset size (remember that ImageNet has a thousand classes). The resulting concepts are then aggregated together from all classes and compared with each other. On the other hand, this approach explicitly relies on the data being labelled, and it may considerably waste energy by recomputing shared concepts present across the different classes of the dataset.

And this is the crux of the matter: I wanted to try a more holistic--and perhaps more ambitious--approach, to make a dictionary with all concepts computed at once on the whole dataset, regardless of there being labels available or not. The clear drawback of the paper's leveraged ADMM algorithm is that it requires storing the complete explained dataset, i.e. the attributions (a.k.a. codes) in memory during the solving procedure. To make matters worse, the ADMM algorithm needs duplicating the variables to account for the separation of optimization objective and constraint enforcement.

This may be feasible on each individual label slice of approximately 1,200 images, even on a beefy laptop because it's separable, but ADMM does not scale particularly well at that scale. If we were to extend it to the full 1.2-million-image dataset $X$, storing as many latents over 384 dimensions in FP32 numbers already consumes about 2GB in memory. The codes array $U$ would be of the same order of magnitude, yet smaller (since $r < n$). Probably manageable even on most modern laptops even on a 8GB GPU, but we're on the edge: any $\times 4$ scaling on the dataset would probably becomes a dealbreaker on consumer hardware.

My point is that we need a more tractable algorithm, even if it means sacrificing convergence speed or even exactness. We need to *stream the dataset and dispose of the codes*. That in itself, is a novelty to the experiment.

### Online solving procedure

#### Decomposability of dictionary learning
In hindsight, all we are interested in is the final dictionary $\mathbf{W}$ solution, _not_ the full transient decompositions $\mathbf{U}$ of the dataset, which can be recomputed at will once $\mathbf{W}$ is learned and frozen. The problem is separable in terms of the training data, thanks to the following equality:

$$
\|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{UW}^\top \|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \| \mathbf{X}_{[i,:]} - \mathbf{U}_{[i,:]} \mathbf{W}\|_2^2
$$
which goes to show that $\mathbf{W}$ could be conceptually updated by only considering a subset of the dataset elements at a time indexed in $\llbracket 1, n \rrbracket$, akin to how neural networks update their weights by considering an objective computed on small views of the training data at a time.


#### Online Non-Negative Matrix Factorization
So I began looking for an algorithm meeting that criterion. To my knowledge it hasn't been attempted on this scale in computer vision research on SOTA models latents.

It turns out that this problem is addressed in the literature. I found a paper about exactly that [@onlinelearning2009] exposing a procedure for online dictionary learning. By the way, Julien Mairal is also a credited author in all three DINO papers. Small world. They address the following problem:

$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, \alpha \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times n}} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{D} \alpha \|_F^2 + \lambda \|\alpha\|_{1,1} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{D} \in \mathcal{C}
$$
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the convex set of matrices whose column vectors belong to the unit ball for the $L_2$ Euclidean norm: $\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf{D}\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, \forall j \in \llbracket 1, k \rrbracket, \mathbf{d}_j^\top \mathbf{d}_j \le 1\}$.

:::note
From this point on, I'll momentarily mix and cite both papers formulas, depending on which one is the most relevant for each paragraph (also because I was too lazy to choose one over the other and I wanted to remain close to the source material). When quoting @onlinelearning2009 (its objective and its algorithm), I'll keep its notation, with data points and codes stored as columns. Everywhere else, I'll stick to the conventions introduced earlier, with data points stored as rows. The only symbol the two share is the code vector of a single data point $\mathbf{h}$, because a single vector looks the same in both conventions (namely it is a column of $\alpha$ on one side and a row of $\mathbf{U}$ on the other).

I'm sorry for this mess. Notations are a hassle and papers love disagreeing and shuffling things around. Also, memory contiguity is a programming constraint thrown into the mix.

For the sake of clarity I laid out the equivalences so you don't have to:

| Meaning | @onlinelearning2009 | @holistic2023fel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rank (or dictionary size) | $k$ | $r$ |
| Data dimensionality | $m$ | $p$ |
| Dataset size | $n$ | $n$ |
| Dictionary (learned weights of interest) | $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$ | $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times r}$ |
| Assignments (or codes) | $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$ | $\mathbf{U}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ |
| Single code vector | $\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$ (column of $\alpha$) | $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{U}_{[i,:]} \in \mathbb{R}^{r}$ |
| Data matrix | $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ | $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ |
:::

The two main differences with our objective are the search space and the regularization term. To begin  with, they use the unit ball for the $\|\cdot\|_{2,\infty}$ column-wise norm in lieu of the orthant of pointwise non-negative elements, and they regularize the assignments $\alpha$ with the $L_1$ norm to favor sparse solutions. Regularizing the weights also avoids degeneracy in the solutions due to the degree of freedom of rescaling reciprocally $\alpha$ and $\mathbf{W}$.

They come up with an algorithm with proven convergence. The said algorithm is reproduced below:


```algorithm
Algorithm: Online Dictionary Learning [@JMLRv11mairal10a]
Require $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \sim p(\mathbf{x})$, $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{D}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$, $T$
$\mathbf{A}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k} \gets \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{B}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k} \gets \mathbf{0}$
For $t = 1$ to $T$
  Draw $\mathbf{x}_t$ from $p(\mathbf{x})$
  State Sparse coding: compute $\mathbf{h}_t$ such that
    $$
    \mathbf{h}_t \triangleq \arg\min_{\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^k} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{D}_{t-1}\mathbf{h}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{h}\|_1
    $$
  $\mathbf{A}_t \gets \mathbf{A}_{t-1} + \mathbf{h}_t \mathbf{h}_t^T$
  $\mathbf{B}_t \gets \mathbf{B}_{t-1} + \mathbf{x}_t \mathbf{h}_t^T$
  State Update dictionary $\mathbf{D}_t$ with $\mathbf{D}_{t-1}$ as a warm restart
    $$
    \mathbf{D}_t \triangleq \arg\min_{\mathbf{D} \in \mathcal{C}} \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t \left(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}_i\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{h}_i\|_1\right) \\
	= \arg\min_{\mathbf{D} \in \mathcal{C}} \frac{1}{t} \left( \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{D}^\top \mathbf{DA}_t - \mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{DB}_t) \right)
    $$
  Return $\mathbf{D}_T$
EndFor
```

```algorithm
Algorithm: Dictionary Update [@JMLRv11mairal10a]
Require $\mathbf{D} = [\mathbf{d}_1,\dots,\mathbf{d}_k]\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$ (input dictionary)
$\mathbf{A} = [\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_k]\in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$
$\mathbf{B} = [\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_k]\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$
Repeat
  For $j = 1$ to $k$
    State Update the $j$-th column:
      $\mathbf{u}_j \leftarrow \big(\mathbf{b}_j - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{a}_j\big) / \mathbf{A}[j,j] + \mathbf{d}_j$
      $\mathbf{d}_j \leftarrow \mathbf{u}_j / \max\big(\left\|\mathbf{u}_j\right\|_2, 1\big)$
  EndFor
Until convergence
Return $\mathbf{D}$ (updated dictionary)
```

So, looking into the procedure reveals that the optimization performs a step in two stages given a batch:

- first, compute the codes, or assignments, of the elements in the batch
- then aggregate statistics about the codes into two buffers and update the dictionary state


In an extended journal version of the paper [@JMLRv11mairal10a], the authors also tackle regularization variants, notably the non-negative matrix factorization problem (NMF for short) taken from another referenced line of research[^note-nmf], as part of the broader framework of different constraint sets for the dictionary and the codes.

[^note-nmf]: That's where we can learn, among other things, that non-negative matrix factorization for sparse coding seems to date back as early as 2001.

They mention the alternative constraint set

$$
\mathcal{C}^\prime = \{\mathbf{D}\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, \forall j \in \llbracket 1, k \rrbracket, \mathbf{d}_j^\top \mathbf{d}_j \le 1 \text{ and } \mathbf{d}_j \ge 0\}
$$

that applies to $\mathbf{D}$, as well as the eventuality of also constraining $\alpha \ge 0$ independently. More importantly, they mention the simplest problem featuring the same objective solely with positivity constraints on $\mathbf{D}$ and $\alpha$ (no norm constraints on the atoms $\mathbf{d}_j$), thus amounting to solve $\arg\min_{\mathbf{D}, \alpha} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{D} \alpha \|_F^2 + \lambda \|\alpha\|_{1,1}$ such that $\mathbf{D}, \alpha \ge 0$. One comment about this particular variant is interesting: 
 
 > "When $\lambda = 0$, this formulation is equivalent to NMF. The only difference with the dictionary learning
problem is that non-negativity constraints are imposed on $\mathbf{D}$ and the vectors $\alpha_i$. A simple modification of our algorithm (...) allows us to handle these constraints, while guaranteeing to find a stationary point of the optimization problem."

Wonderful. This is exactly the setup we're looking for. The implications here affect both steps of the overall procedure, namely the initial sparse coding solve step (by constraining additionally $\mathbf{h}$ to remain non-negative), and the projections in the dictionary update routine.


The paper describes a framework where the dictionary can be learned by only streaming one dataset element at a time, but the algorithm trivially generalizes to batch sizes above 1. 



### Dictionary learning hot path
The numerical computations of the dictionary learning rely heavily on vectorized operations and linear algebra but no differentiation is involved, so they are a straightforward fit for [Jax](https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/) which provides first-class compilation for all sorts of hardware, including CUDA, of course. This is especially handy for this kind of iterative algorithms to solve linear algebra problems. Before this project I was familiar with PyTorch but I had never used Jax so it was a good incentive to try and learn.

In my code, I kept the original notations with $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times r}$ and $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$. However, since $\mathbf{U}$ is conceptually reserved for the full dataset codes, and is never materialized in memory, I named its batched counterpart $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times r}$ where $b$ is the batch size with obviously $b < n$. At any step, we draw $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times p}$ and apply the above update step with two stages.

Conceptually the main training loop looks like this:

```python
# import jax.numpy as jnp

def train_step(
    W: jnp.ndarray,   # (DATA_DIM, DICT_SIZE)
    A: jnp.ndarray,   # (DICT_SIZE, DICT_SIZE)
    B: jnp.ndarray,   # (DATA_DIM, DICT_SIZE)
    X: jnp.ndarray,   # (BATCH_SIZE, DATA_DIM)
    H0: jnp.ndarray,  # (BATCH_SIZE, DICT_SIZE)
    T: int,           # global step counter
    lam: float,       # regularization weight
) -> tuple[jnp.ndarray, ...]:
    H = nonnegative_lasso_fista(W=W, X=X, lam=lam, H0=H0)
    W, A, B = update_dictionary(W=W, H=H, X=X, A=A, B=B, T=T)
    return W, A, B
```

Overall, I parametrized a lot more than meets the eye in this example snippet, to make it possible to change a few hyperparameters:

- maximum allowed radius of a concept vector
- non-negativity constraint on the concept atoms (synced to incoming latent's negative entries clamping)
- maximum number of passes and tolerance threshold for the FISTA to solve the LASSO
- number of dictionary update steps 

#### Non-negative LASSO sparse coding step
The modified sparse positive coding step to compute

$$
\mathbf{h}_t = \arg\min_{\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^k} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{D}_{t-1}\mathbf{h}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{h}\|_1
$$
at every step is a procedure in and of itself. In principle, one could simply call a generic convex optimization solver for this kind of problem, but such solvers almost all run on the CPU. Calling a generic solver at every step is a poor fit for this setting, for two obvious reasons. First, as I said, the solver would execute on the CPU, while the dictionary update and the statistics aggregation run on the CUDA device with Jax. Every iteration would therefore pay a slow PCIe transfer in both directions. Second, the interoperability of generic MILP solvers with Jax is nonexistent. Just assembling the constraint matrix and the objective term would mean copying large buffers, which is expensive in memory. Together, these costs would make it hard to sustain several exact dictionary-update iterations per second.

So, we're better off using an iterative, approximate algorithm to solve the non-negative LASSO step. With the help of an LLM, I found a respectable algorithm that lends itself well to the NN-LASSO problem, called Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA) [@teboulle09fista]. To be honest, I wasn't particularly interested in the details of this minor technicality in the broader scheme of dictionary learning, so I gladly let Codex do the research and work out the Jax function for it. The FISTA procedure is an algorithm that projects a partial solution onto a convex set after every gradient step. The bundle of a gradient step followed by convex projection is called a "proximal operator". A relevant practicality to be aware of is that it involves an iterative procedure of its own, so it needs tuning, and a stopping criterion. A caveat is that the norm of the proximal gradient steps does not usually decrease monotonically, so setting a threshold on the step size is not the best of stopping criteria. However, because this iterative procedure is itself an intermediate result to the overall output of the dictionary learning problem, and because transient dictionary states constantly warm up the solution to the next batch and step, it barely matters anyway.

Also, instead of simply summing the statistics in matrices $A$ and $B$, which could blow up numerical accuracy with large numbers, I accumulated them using the online average update formula to avoid overflow. The update of every $j$-th column in the subsequent `for` loop is invariant to rescaling $A$ and $B$ by a common factor so it's fine.


#### Dictionary update step

The dictionary update step is pretty much about following the  instructions from the above algorithm to the letter, with the additional projection to enforce non-negative values for the new dictionary columns. I kept the original column-wise $L_2$ unit ball constraint for stability of the dictionary atoms[^note-proj-l2-lasso-fista], and  obviously, I added the projection onto the set of positively-valued vectors.

[^note-proj-l2-lasso-fista]: Without it, the concept vectors would explode and their norms would increase indefinitely in my tests.


At a later stage prototyping and benchmarking my code on the GPU, I had also Claude suggest using a vectorized Jacobi update scaling by $A$'s largest eigenvalue, instead of the proposed blocking Gauss-Seidel `for` loop.

### Decisive design choices

#### Training
Apart from solving hyperparameters under the hood of the solving procedure (which can be abstracted away from the declarative problem statement's point of view), only a few hyperparameters will decisively affect the dictionary:
- the semantics of the sourced inputs, as stated above
- the size of the learned dictionary $r$
- the sparsity weight $\lambda$

#### Inference

Likewise, after concepts are frozen and we use the concepts to reconstruct some latents, actually deciding what procedure to retain to compute the codes from a vector is subject to discussion. There are two possibilities:
- solve the relaxed dense sparse LASSO problem again, which requires setting the sparsity weight $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}_+$: it's the LASSO problem that has already been written numerous times above:
$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^k_+} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{h}\|_1
$$
- solve a combinatorial sparse MIQP least squares problem, which requires setting the atom sarsity budget $s \in \mathbb{N}$:
$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^k_+} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\|_2^2 \quad \text{ s.t. } \quad \|\mathbf{h}\|_0 \le s
$$
(the constraint counts the number of non-zero elements in $\mathbf{h}$)





### Quality evaluation
The third stage is about probing the produced dictionary with relevant metrics to test whether it is *meaningful* and *descriptive* enough, that is, whether the dictionary conveys a sense of *completeness* to describe the data.

#### Hoyer sparsity

At the scale of an individual concept level, the _Hoyer sparsity measure_ [@nmf2004hoyer] for the corresponding latent vector $\mathbf{d}$ in dimension $n$ is defined as follows:
$$
\mathrm{Hoyer}(\mathbf{d})
= \frac{\sqrt{n} - \frac{\|\mathbf{d}\|_1}{\|\mathbf{d}\|_2}}{\sqrt{n} - 1}
$$

#### Residuals

We can evaluate the residual between a latent $\mathbf{x}$ and its reconstruction $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x})$:
$$
\text{Residual}(\mathbf{x}) = \| \mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}} \|
$$
where $\mathbf{h}$ is the (approximate) reconstruction codes from the iterative procedure. The exact norm flavor is intentionally unspecified, it can be scaled $L_2$ (MSE), RMSE or even $L_\infty$ depending on the focus.

The residuals can be aggregated into a single normalized quantity in $[0,1]$ called the *fraction of variance unexplained*, which is just the complement of what a PCA does:

$$
\text{FVU} = \frac{\sum_i \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}_i\|_2^2}{\sum_i \|\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}\|^2_2} \in [0, 1]
$$

For the sake of simplicity, since the last layers of DINOv3 models are `LayerNorm`, I'll consider that the space of all possible image latents is whitened and that their mean vector $\bar{\mathbf{x}}$ is zero (even though that of a specific dataset might not exactly be centered).

#### Entropy
Finally, given reconstruction codes $\mathbf{h} \ge 0$, we may reinterpret it as a probability distribution over concepts atoms. So I came up with a normalized entropy score that acts like a soft measurement of dispersion for the codes:
$$
\text{let} \quad p = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{h}\|_1}\mathbf{h} \quad \text{so that} \quad \tilde{H}(\mathbf{h}) = \frac{1}{\log k}H(p)
$$
where $H$ is Shannon's entropy.

In @onlinelearning2009, it is specified that in practice, less than a handful of dictionary update steps are needed for the convergence to happen properly (they mention 3 updates needed for experimental success). Following that idea, I set the loop to run a fixed number of times.


Likewise, the FISTA loop to solve the sparse coding assignments in the first stage of a training step stops once the proximal gradient step barely moves the assignments anymore. The chosen criterion proxy is the ratio of Frobenius norms of the current proximal gradient norm to the initial proximal gradient norm in the batch step. As I stated earlier, it's not perfect, but it's a good enough heuristic for measuring tracking convergence.

And yes, this is not a typo, I did set $\lambda$ to 0 in the end. It helps producing orthogonal concepts.

Remember that the whole point of a sparse decomposition is hypothesizing that the data lies in some lower-dimensional linear manifold of the latent space. That lead me to consider 256 atoms in the dictionary, about a quarter of the 1000 classes of ImageNet-1K. Since ImageNet-1K is very self-similar when seen through DINOv3 (see the distribution of pairwise cosine similarities figure from a [previous post](/posts/imagenet-hidden-hierarchy/#fig-pairwise-imagenet-cosine), it's computed on the ConvNeXt backbone version, but the argument holds for the ViT's: ImageNet-1K is not very diverse in the end), that seemed reasonable.

But what about the advertised fraction of variance unexplained? Arguably, in reality, assignments obtained by means of a sparse LASSO problem requires to choose a value for $\lambda$ and it's hard to make sense of it intuitively. Moreover, $\lambda$ controls sparsity only indirectly: nothing guarantees how many atoms a given code will actually use. 

So let's look at the codes from the exact MIQP formulation instead, where the budget $s$ caps the number of active concepts directly. <a data-figure-ref="fig-fvu-by-concept-budget"></a> shows the per-sample unexplained energy $\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{h}\|_2^2 / \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$ for budgets of at most 1, 2 and 5 concepts, respectively. Since the latents are assumed centered, aggregating it over the samples gives the fraction of variance unexplained.

A single concept typically explains already about a third of the variance, on average. Allowing two brings that to almost 40%, and five to roughly 50%. So the returns tend to diminish quickly: each additional concept explains less than the one before, and even with five, half of the signal remains unexplained.
