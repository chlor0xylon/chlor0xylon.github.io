This is the second part of a series on reproducing recent advancements in computer vision interpretability. In the [first part](/posts/dinov3-prototypes/), I introduced the history of feature visualization. Here, I attempt to reproduce a dictionary learning approach on the DINOv3 model.

## Dictionary learning: an introduction

Before visualizing concepts as images, we need to identify semantically representative directions in the model's latent space to gain better understanding of how visuals are encoded. This can be achieved thanks to a prominent method in ML interpretability research called _dictionary learning_.

Broadly speaking, dictionary learning seeks to discover a set of building blocks to express complex data as a sparse combinations of these patterns--sometimes refered to as _atoms_--that together form the _dictionary_. An efficient dictionary should provide compact, meaningful representations that are useful for interpretability purposes, denoising, or feature extraction for example. Unlike PCA or SVD, dictionary learning approaches usually attempt to make the decomposition _sparse_. In that sense, it is close in spirit to data compression. Because of that, dictionary learning is also known as _sparse coding_.

:::note
Data compression works by seeking a representation basis to encode the data in the most compact way possible. For example, the JPEG standard breaks down each $8 \times 8$ block of pixels into a sum of cosine-based waveforms. It turns out that real-world image signals are usually better explained by smooth variations resembling a sparse combination of cosine waves, and this better prior it allows a lower-rank representation to match human perceptual vision with fewer resources. Sparsity is a desirable property when doing coding, either for storage saving purposes, or for the sake of interpretability.
:::


### Formal definition
I will rely on @holistic2023fel, a paper that reviews the concept extraction variants attempts in the literature and compares them all and identifies their commonalities. One of the latest methods presented was coined CRAFT, standing for _Concept Recursive Activation FacTorization for Explainability_ and was taken from the preceding paper @craft2023fel, which adds non-negativity constraint to the learned priors. In the paper, it is claimed indeed that the positivity of the decomposition 
- "encourages sparsity"
- "allows missing data to be imputed"
- "enhances the interpretability of the decomposition"

:::note
The first claim is obvious because negative entries will force the concept vectors to keep many null entries to be able to encode zero entries, the closest approximation of any negative value projected onto the half-line $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$. Regarding the second claim, the authors develop an attribution method for visualizing the concept activations in images and rely on preceeding research to support their statement, but here I'm only interested in the part about dictionary learning, and I don't know or care about it for now. Regarding the third one, my understanding of the approach is that only positive activations are considered meaningful because they represent strongly-typed features, whereas negative activations do not carry significant information similar to how negative logit values would compress into irrelevancy and produce near-zero probabilities that are overshadowed by positive logits.
:::

The setup goes like this: let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$ be a set of $n$ captured activations in dimension $p$ from the latents from a vision model taken from a provided collection of images. Let $r$ be a positive integer, denoted as the rank of the decomposition. The positive dictionary learning approach consists in solving the following problem:

$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{U}\in \mathbb{R}^{n\times r}, \, \mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{p\times r}} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{UW}^\top \|_F^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{W}, \mathbf{U} \ge 0
$$

where $\mathbf{W}$ is the _dictionary of concepts_ onto which the dataset is projected and $\mathbf{U}$ is the dataset decomposition, i.e. the assignments of each data point to each of the $r$ concepts. For dictionary learning to be meaningful, $r$ and $n$ must be chosen such that $r < n$.

Note that $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{W}$ are required to belong to the positive orthant of matrices with **non-negative entries**. That constraint is a deliberate modeling choice. Note that the solution space is convex, but the search is made arduous because positivity prevents the use of off-the-shelf linear algebra results. This formulated problem  is known as the **Non-Negative Least Squares problem** (NNLS), and I first encountered during my Master's Thesis in the context of beamforming in transmission-related problems.


### Naïve solving procedure
Solving the above mathematical problem can be approached in at least a few different manners. In Fel's PhD thesis [@fel2025sparksexplainabilityrecentadvancements], section C.1.3 of the manuscript along the implementation details of CRAFT, they explain that they used a method called alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM for short), which I also encountered during my Master's Thesis, because it arises a lot in the literature about applied mathematical optimization problems with cumbersome constraints such as non-negativity. There is no point outlining ADMM int his post, as it is better explained in numerous other papers, articles and posts freely available, and I won't use it anyway.


### Toward a more holistic approach
@holistic2023fel published an implementation of the CRAFT method resulting in an interactive website called the [Lens Project](https://serre-lab.github.io/Lens/) that allows to navigate and visualize them. They only retain a few major concepts per class which sums to quite a large pool, but they did not actually compute them across the whole dataset at once, as far as I understand. Rather, the dictionary is computed on a per-class basis and is low-rank from the start to only retain a handful of concepts per class (they take 10). In a later stage, their attribution method yields the contribution scores to each class.


On one hand, this significantly lowers the numerical complexity requirements since it cuts by a factor of 1000 the effective dataset size (remember that ImageNet has a thousand classes). The resulting concepts are then aggregated together from all classes and compared with each other. On the other hand, this approach explicitly relies on the data being labelled, and it may considerably waste energy by recomputing shared concepts present across the different classes of the dataset.

And this is the crux of the matter: I wanted to try a more holistic--and perhaps more ambitious--approach, to make a dictionary with all concepts computed at once on the whole dataset, regardless of there being labels available or not. The clear drawback of the paper's leveraged ADMM algorithm is that it requires storing the complete explained dataset, i.e. the attributions (a.k.a. codes) in memory during the solving procedure. To make matters worse, the ADMM algorithm needs duplicating the variables to account for the separation of optimization objective and constraint enforcement.

This may be feasible on each individual label slice of approximately 1,200 images, even on a beefy laptop because it's separable, but ADMM does not scale particularly well beyond that milestone. If we were to extend it to the full 1.2-million-image dataset $X$, storing as many latents over 384 dimensions in FP32 numbers already consumes about 2GB in memory. The codes array $U$ would be of the same order of magnitude, yet smaller (since $r < n$). Probably manageable even on most modern laptops even on a 8GB GPU, but we're on the edge: any $\times 4$ scaling on the dataset would probably becomes a dealbreaker on consumer hardware.

My point is that we need a more tractable algorithm, even if it means sacrifying convergence speed or even exactness. Wee need to *stream the dataset and dispose of the codes*. That in itself, is a novelty to the experiment.

### Online solving procedure

#### Decomposability of dictionary learning
In hindsight, all we are intereseted in is the final dictionary $\mathbf{W}$ solution, _not_ the full transient decompositions $\mathbf{U}$ of the dataset, which can be recomputed at will once $\mathbf{W}$ is learned and frozen. The problem is separable in terms of the training data, thanks to the following equality:

$$
\|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{UW}^\top \|_F^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \| \mathbf{X}_{[i,:]} - \mathbf{U}_{[i,:]} \mathbf{W}\|_2^2
$$
which goes to show that $\mathbf{W}$ could be conceptually updated by only considering a subset of the dataset elements at a time indexed in $\llbracket 1, n \rrbracket$, akin to how neural networks update their weights by considering an objective computed on small views of the training data at a time.


#### Online Non-Negative Matrix Factorization
So I began looking for an algorithm meeting that criterion. To my knowledge it hasn't been attempted on this scale in computer vision research on SOTA models latents.

It turns out that this problem is addressed in the literature. I found a paper about exactly that [@onlinelearning2009] exposing a procedure for online dictionary learning. By the way, Julien Mairal is also a credited author in all three DINO papers. Small world. They address the following problem:

$$
\arg\min_{\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, \alpha \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times n}} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{D} \alpha \|_F^2 + \lambda \|\alpha\|_{1,1} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{D} \in \mathcal{C}
$$
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the convex set of matrices whose column vectors belong to the unit ball for the $L_2$ Euclidian norm: $\mathcal{C} = \{\mathbf{D}\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, \forall j \in \llbracket 1, k \rrbracket, \mathbf{d}_j^\top \mathbf{d}_j \le 1\}$.

:::note
For the sake of clarity, I momentarily adopted their original notations, but here are equivalences between their conventions and the ones earlier:

| Meaning | @onlinelearning2009 | Above |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rank (or dictionary size) | $k$ | $r$ |
| Data dimensionality | $m$ | $p$ |
| Dataset size | $n$ | $n$ |
| Dictionary (or learned weights of interest) | $\mathbf{D} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$ | $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times r}$ |
| Assignments (or codes) | $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$ | $\mathbf{U}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n}$ |
| Data matrix | $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ | $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ |
:::

The two main differences with our objective are the search space and the regularization term. To begin  with, they use the unit ball for the $\|\cdot\|_{2,\infty}$ column-wise norm in lieu of the orthant of poitwise non-negative elements, and they regularize the assignments $\alpha$ with the $L_1$ norm to favor sparse solutions. Regularizing the weights also avoids degeneracy in the solutions due to the degree of freedom of rescaling recoprocally $\alpha$ and $\mathbf{W}$.

They come up with an algorithm with proven convergence. The said algorithm is reproduced below:


```algorithm
Algorithm: Online Dictionary Learning [@JMLRv11mairal10a]
Require $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^m \sim p(\mathbf{x})$, $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, $\mathbf{D}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$, $T$
$\mathbf{A}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k} \gets \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{B}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k} \gets \mathbf{0}$
For $t = 1$ to $T$
  Draw $\mathbf{x}_t$ from $p(\mathbf{x})$
  State Sparse coding: compute $\alpha_t$ such that
    $$
    \alpha_t \triangleq \arg\min_{\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^k} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{D}_{t-1}\alpha\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\alpha\|_1
    $$
  $\mathbf{A}_t \gets \mathbf{A}_{t-1} + \alpha_t \alpha_t^T$
  $\mathbf{B}_t \gets \mathbf{B}_{t-1} + \mathbf{x}_t \alpha_t^T$
  State Update dictionary $\mathbf{D}_t$ with $\mathbf{D}_{t-1}$ as a warm restart
    $$
    \mathbf{D}_t \triangleq \arg\min_{\mathbf{D} \in \mathcal{C}} \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t \left(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{D}\alpha_i\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\alpha_i\|_1\right) \\
	= \arg\min_{\mathbf{D} \in \mathcal{C}} \frac{1}{t} \left( \frac{1}{2} \mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{D}^\top \mathbf{DA}_t - \mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{DB}_t) \right)
    $$
  Return $\mathbf{D}_T$
EndFor
```

```algorithm
Algorithm: Dictionary Update [@JMLRv11mairal10a]
Require $\mathbf{D} = [\mathbf{d}_1,\dots,\mathbf{d}_k]\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$ (input dictionary)
$\mathbf{A} = [\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_k]\in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k}$
$\mathbf{B} = [\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_k]\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}$
Repeat
  For $j = 1$ to $k$
    State Update the $j$-th column:
      $\mathbf{u}_j \leftarrow \big(\mathbf{b}_j - \mathbf{D}\mathbf{a}_j\big) / \mathbf{A}[j,j] + \mathbf{d}_j$
      $\mathbf{d}_j \leftarrow \mathbf{u}_j / \max\big(\left\|\mathbf{u}_j\right\|_2, 1\big)$
  EndFor
Until convergence
Return $\mathbf{D}$ (updated dictionary)
```

So, looking into the procedure reveals that the optimization performs a step in two stages given a batch:

- first, compute the codes, or assignments, of the elements in the batch
- then aggregate statistics about the codes into two buffers and update the dictionary state


In an extended journal version of the paper [@JMLRv11mairal10a], the authors also tackle regularization variants, notably the non-negative matrix factorization problem (NMF for short) taken from another referenced line of research[^note-nmf], as part of the broader framework of different constraint sets for the dictionary and the codes.

[^note-nmf]: That's where we can learn, among other things, that non-negative matrix factorization for sparse coding seems to date back as early as 2001.

They mention the alternative constraint set

$$
\mathcal{C}^\prime = \{\mathbf{D}\in \mathbb{R}^{m\times k}, \forall j \in \llbracket 1, k \rrbracket, \mathbf{d}_j^\top \mathbf{d}_j \le 1 \text{ and } \mathbf{d}_j \ge 0\}
$$

that applies to $\mathbf{D}$, as well as the eventuality of also constraining $\alpha \ge 0$ independently. More importantly, they mention the simplest problem featuring the same objective solely with positivity constraints on $\mathbf{D}$ and $\alpha$ (no norm constraints on the atoms $\mathbf{d}_j$), thus amounting to solve $\arg\min_{\mathbf{D}, \alpha} \|\mathbf{X} - \mathbf{D} \alpha \|_F^2 + \lambda \|\alpha\|_{1,1}$ such that $\mathbf{D}, \alpha \ge 0$. One comment about this particular variant is interesting: 
 
 > "When $\lambda = 0$, this formulation is equivalent to NMF. The only difference with the dictionary learning
problem is that non-negativity constraints are imposed on $\mathbf{D}$ and the vectors $\alpha_i$. A simple modification of our algorithm (...) allows us to handle these constraints, while guaranteeing to find a stationary point of the optimization problem."

Wonderful. This is exactly the setup we're looking for. The implications here affect both steps of the overall procedure, namely the initial sparse coding solve step (by constraining additionally $\alpha$ to remain non-negative), and the projections in the dictionary update routine.


The paper describes a framework where the dictionary can be learned by only streaming one dataset element at a time, but the algorithm trivially generalizes to batch sizes above 1. 


## Methodology & implementation details

In this section, I'm introducing the main steps of happening throughout the process of computing prototypes as well as some implementation details.


In order to get concepts and visualize them altogether according to the approach proposed by @holistic2023fel, I wrote several Python scripts that given a Hugging Face model and a dataset, perform in turn the following steps:
- A custom `ImageNetDataset(torch.Dataset)`
    - scrapes and indexes images from the downloaded ImageNet dataset file tree structure as well as some metadata like image size and saves them in a Polars dataframe to emulate a tiny database;
    - filters images to select those whose dimensions lie above 64 pixels in both width and height (which only eliminates a negligible proportion of the dataset, see <a data-figure-ref="fig-imagenet-density"></a>);
    - generates and appends some random augmentation transform codes to the batch generated by a custom-made `TransformsMaker` factory (for example, in our case setting, transform codes model random crops to extract concepts from intermediate-sized image regions).


- A processing loop streams those formed batches to the model adapter, which extracts their latent CLS token or pooled outputs and repackages the model outputs, unique ids and transform codes to be replayed later upon retrieval.
- A custom-made `ArrayArtifactStore` persists the raw batched model outputs as binary numpy format sharded them into batches on disk, and saves the metadata transform codes and ids to a Polars bookkeeping registry.
- A specialized dictionary learning script iteratively updates the dictionary weights and persists them on disk, relying on:
    - a custom new `VectorDataset(torch.Dataset)` with manually-paginated lazy loading capability via `__getitems__` to leverage OS-level file caching instead of making one (or using numpy's memory map) and decouple the requested materialization batch size in memory from the sharding size on disk;
    - yet another dataloader wrapper `VectorDataLoader(torch.DataLoader)`.
- A concept visualizer script is responsible for
    - reading the concepts saved on disk;
    - triggering a custom-made `VectorDataset` method that leverages [Usearch](https://github.com/unum-cloud/usearch) to build a compacted vector index to be able to compute efficient matching & retrieval later;
    - retrieving the closest matches in the dataset's saved latents (in terms of cosine distance) for every concept vector of the dictionary;
    - reloading images from the disk then replaying their transformations and finally displaying them.

### Dictionary learning hot path
The numerical computations of the dictionary learning rely heavily on vectorized operations and linear algebra but no differenciation is involved, so they are a straightforward fit for [Jax](https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/) which provides first-class compilation for all sorts of hardware, incuding CUDA, of course. This is especially handy for this kind of iterative algorithms to solve linear algebra problems. Before this project I was familiar with PyTorch but I had never used Jax so it was a good incentive to try and learn.

In my code, I kept the original notations with $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times r}$ and $\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$. However, since $\mathbf{U}$ is conceptually reserved for the full dataset codes, and is never materialized in memory, I named its batched counterpart $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times r}$ where $b$ is the batch size with obviously $b < n$. At any step, we draw $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times n}$ and apply the above update step with two stages.

Conceptually the main training loop looks like this:

```python
# import jax.numpy as jnp

def train_step(
    W: jnp.ndarray,   # (DATA_DIM, DICT_SIZE)
    A: jnp.ndarray,   # (DICT_SIZE, DICT_SIZE)
    B: jnp.ndarray,   # (DATA_DIM, DICT_SIZE)
    X: jnp.ndarray,   # (BATCH_SIZE, DATA_DIM)
    H0: jnp.ndarray,  # (BATCH_SIZE, DICT_SIZE)
    T: int,           # global step counter
    lam: float,       # regularization weight
) -> tuple[jnp.ndarray, ...]:
    H = nonnegative_lasso_fista(W=W, X=X, lam=lam, H0=H0)
    W, A, B = update_dictionary(W=W, H=H, X=X, A=A, B=B, T=T)
    return W, A, B
```

#### Non-negative LASSO sparse coding step
The modified sparse positive coding step to compute $\alpha_t = \arg\min_{\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^k} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{D}_{t-1}\alpha\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\alpha\|_1$ is a procedure in and of itself. In principle, onc could simply call a generic convex optimization solver for this kind of problem, but such solvers almost all run on the CPU. Calling a generic solver at every step is a poor fit for this setting, for two obvious reasons. First, as I said, the solver would execute on the CPU, while the dictionary update and the statistics aggregation run on the CUDA device with Jax. Every iteration would therefore pay a slow PCIe transfer in both directions. Second, the interoperability of generic MILP solvers with Jax is nonexistent. Just assembling the constraint matrix and the objective term would mean copying large buffers, which is expensive in memory. Together, these costs would make it hard to sustain several exact dictionary-update iterations per second.

So, we're better off using an iterative, approximate algorithm to solve the non-negative LASSO step. With the help of an LLM, I found a respectable algorithm that lends itself well to the NN-LASSO problem, called Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA) [@teboulle09fista]. To be honest, I wasn't particularly interested in the details of this minor technicality in the broader scheme of dictionary learning, so I gladly let Codex do the research and work out the Jax function for it. The only relevant practicality to be aware of is that it involves an iterative procedure of its own, so we'll need to tuning the tolerance and iteration cap.

Also, instead of simply summing the statistics in matrices $A$ and $B$, which could blow up numerical accuracy with large numbers, I accumulated them using the online average update formula to avoid overflow. The update of every $j$-th column in the subsequent `for` loop is invariant to rescaling $A$ and $B$ by a common factor so it's fine.


#### Dictionary update step

The dictionary update step is pretty much about following the  instructions from the above algorithm to the letter, with the additional projection to enforce non-negative values for the new dictionary columns. I kept the original columnn-wise $L_2$ unit ball constraint for stability of the dictionary atoms[^note-proj-l2-lasso-fista], and  obviously, I added the projection onto the set of positively-valued vectors.

[^note-proj-l2-lasso-fista]: Without it, the concept vectors would explode and their norms would increase indefinitely in my tests.


At a later stage prototyping and benchmarking my code on the GPU, I had also Claude suggest using a vectorized Jacobi update scaling by $A$'s largest eigenvalue, instead of the proposed blocking Gauss-Seidel `for` loop.




## Protocol design choices

Let's outline some global design choices of the dictionary learning method (see also <a data-figure-ref="fig-online-dictionary-learning-svg"></a>). The values below are preliminary settings meant to be explored and to illustrate their impact on the statistics of the resulting concepts; they are not necessarily the final values I retained.


### Obtaining crops

The first stage is to compute image embeddings from image crops of the dataset. I discarded images with width or height less than 128 pixels. Then I ran a script that extracts random crops of size $128 \times 128$ pixels from images of the dataset, runs them through the vision encoder, and persists the obtained latents on the disk. Other augmentations are of course possible (like geometry or colorimetry alterations) to capture more elaborate concepts about the model's internal representations, but I did not use any of these so far. Maybe another time. I ran several passes on the dataset with different seeds, to obtain different views of all images in the dataset.

My idea is to train the dictionary on the latents obtained from views of images that encapsulate extended local visual context compared to prior results from the literature. The 128 pixels size is arbitrary, but it remains somewhat consistent with the dataset's image width and height distribution: ImageNet contains many images of small resolution by modern image standards (see <a data-figure-ref="fig-imagenet-density"></a>). In @craft2023fel, 64-by-64-pixel patches are used, so  bumping it up to 128 seems like a sound middleground to extract local semantics all the while extending the field of view to a limited fraction of the image in the majority of cases (see <a data-figure-ref="fig-imagenet-crops-area-ratio-svg"></a>).

### Dictionary learning design choices
The second stage consists of running the dictionary learning script on the persisted batches of latent vectors on a dictionary buffer of $k=512$ concepts, and execute the training algorithm in the above code, with LASSO sparse coding regularization weight $\lambda$ set to 0. At this point, the objective is not to get sparse codes as of yet, but rather to compute a dictionary from diverse, entangled concepts without a sparsity constraint, that's why I disabled the regularization entirely. The reason I went with 512 concepts for now is threefold: it is a power of two for memory alignment if anything; it is about half the number of classes in the ImageNet dataset--which may incidentally already form clusters in the model's latent space--so it safely avoids concepts collapsing onto trivial underlying vectors (possibly due to enlarging the crop size); and I hypothesize that it is close to the actual number of "independent" concepts, which features numerous inter-class concept similarities.



### Examining the latent concepts
The third stage is about querying the produced dictionary for relevant metrics to ensure it is well-formed. To do so, I evaluated the codes of unaltered images from the same dataset with a sparsity constraint in order to gauge the bottleneck of the learned dictionary.


### Results

#### Chosen metrics
I'll use three metrics to get a get a sense of the produced concepts.

First, a quick search revealed the _Hoyer sparsity measure_ [@nmf2004hoyer] at the scale of an individual concept vector $\mathbf{d}$ in dimension $n$:
$$
\mathrm{Hoyer}(\mathbf{d})
= \frac{\sqrt{n} - \frac{\|\mathbf{d}\|_1}{\|\mathbf{d}\|_2}}{\sqrt{n} - 1}
$$
Second, we can obviously evaluate the residues of the positive part of a given vector $\mathbf{x}$ once reconstructed by the dictionary:
$$
\text{Residue}(\mathbf{x}) = \||\mathbf{x}| - \mathbf{D} \alpha(\mathbf{x}) \|
$$
where $\alpha$ is the (approximate) reconstruction codes from the iterative procedure. The exact norm flavor is intentionally unspecified, it can be $L_2$ or $L_\infty$ in particular.

Finally, given a reconstruction vector $\alpha$, given that the codes are non-negative, I was also curious to reinterpret it as a probability distribution over concepts. So came up with a normalized entropy score that acts like a measurement of density for the codes:
$$
\text{let} \quad p = \frac{1}{\|\alpha\|_1}\mathbf{\alpha} \quad \text{so that} \quad \tilde{H}(\alpha) = \frac{1}{\log k}H(p)
$$

(recall that $k$ is the dictionary size, thus the dimension of $\alpha$ too).

#### Analyzing the codes

Then I evaluated how ImageNet latents decompose over the learned dictionary by solving the non-negative LASSO coding step. <a data-figure-ref="fig-dictionary-code-distributions"></a> reports the three summaries: normalized code entropy, Hoyer sparsity, and reconstruction residue norms for reconstructions for $\lambda = 0.5$. These plots depict the three chosen sparsity metrics aggregated over a pool of image samples from the ImageNet dataset. All three are unimodal distributions, which is a bit reassuring, in the sense that the contrary would probably raise questions about the stability of the iterative procedure. The residue norms typically lie in the range of a few units. To put that into perspective, we have to remember that it means the error spreads over the 384 latent dimensions of the model (`facebook/dinov3-vits16-pretrain-lvd1689m`), and compare it against the typical value range of the latents dimensions.
