Almost ten years ago, as I was wandering the internet during my bachelor years, I once came across the famous machine learning blog [Distill.pub](https://distill.pub). At the time, this research blog stood at the forefront of research in computer vision interpretability, among other related topics. To some extent it still does in 2026. I can't remember exactly how I stumbled upon it back then, but I wasn't introduced to computer vision or even machine learning basics at that time, and I clearly remember the technicality of it all leaving me intrigued yet mostly confused at the sight of it. I thought to myself: *What are those funny visuals supposed to mean? What exactly do they represent? Mildly interesting, but weird.* Not grasping the significance of this work, I archived the URL nonetheless for future reference.

Lately, I discovered it again by accident in the process of doing a literature review, so I decided to skim through it anew and catch up with the research with the benefit of hindsight, having more time and background knowledge to delve properly into the contents.

## Feature visualization

One of Distill's foundational articles is called _Feature Visualization_ [@olah2017featureVisualization]. It describes how neural networks designed for computer vision tasks can be probed to reverse-engineer the learned patterns cristallized into their weights after the training phase[^note-older-feature-viz].

[^note-older-feature-viz]: Even in 2017, visualizing features by creating images to maximize activations wasn't exactly new. The numerous papers cited in Distill's _Feature Visualization_ is evidence of those previous attempts, some as early as 2009, even before [AlexNet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlexNet)'s breakthrough.

{/* The work carried out by Distill's authors has greatly expanded the use cases in computer vision interpretability that was pioneered along gradual developments in convolutional neural networks to explore ever more specialized feature visualization such as curve detectors [@cammarata2020curveDetectors] or spacial frequency edge detectors [@schubert2021highLowFrequencyDetectors] and using different parametrizations [@mordvintsev2018differentiableParameterizations]. */}

:::note
At the time, the Transformers architecture [@vaswani2017attention] and the [attention](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_(machine_learning)>) mechanism had just barely been conceptualized, and state-of-the-art vision models still used convolutional backbones, stemming from learnable, signal processing-inspired, convolutional filters that underpins modern computer vision. Leveraging Transformers for vision tasks wouldn't be formally introduced for another two years, until 2019 [@dosovitskiy2021vit].
:::

## Reverse-engineering visual patterns

To _visualize_ features in this context means to synthesize images by whichever means possible, comprising patterns that maximize some given activations or outputs of a neural network, in order to gain insights about what the model perceives and how it ``interprets'' the inputs. In other words it's about finding _prototypical_ images that make particular neuron groups fire in a certain way[^note-on-fire-modes]. The proper mathematical term is _preimage_, but it has nothing to do with actual images made of pixels, so to avoid that ambiguity I will henceforth refer to such artificial images simply as _prototypes_ in the remaining of this post.

[^note-on-fire-modes]: Said chosen groups can mean a specific neuron, or a collection of designated neurons. Distill's article _The Building Blocks of Interpretability_ [@olah2018buildingBlocks] showcases some neuron grouping choices for activation maximization.

But one of the simplest ideas to compute _prototype images_ is to treat the model as a (differentiable) black box processing its input space, and the exposed outputs. For mid-2010's-era image classification models such as Microsoft's ResNet [@he2016deepResidualLearning], the model outputs are the logits. For modern state-of-the-art ViT models, that means either patch tokens, the CLS token, register tokens [@darcet2024visionTransformersNeedRegisters], or a mix of those.

To put it plainly, let $f$ be a provided, trained, frozen neural network accepting images in (a subset of) $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^{C \times W \times H}$ in input, that returns output vectors $\mathcal{Y} \ni \mathbf{y} = f(\mathbf{x})$. To find a prototype $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ in input space for some objective function $s: \mathcal{Y} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ means to solve

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}} s(f(\mathbf{x}))
$$

which translates into ``finding an input that represents its class or target latent representation perfectly according to measure $s$''.

For example, $s$ can denote:

- the logit at index $k$: $s(\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{e}_k$ (pre-softmax) in a classification model
- the cosine of the angle between the output CLS token and a target vector $\mathbf{y}_0$ whose semantic meaning we're interested in: $s(\mathbf{y}) = \frac{\mathbf{y} \cdot \mathbf{y}_0}{\|\mathbf{y}\|\|\mathbf{y}_0\|}$, in a general feature extractor vision model
- the activation of a specific internal neuron

In this setup, the optimized variable is the input to the frozen network, a.k.a. the data, as opposed to the training phase where the data was fixed, and drawn from a dataset, and the network's weights were the optimized variables. The point is then to use optimization techniques to synthesize input images that maximally activate specific neurons or layers.

For any neural network of more than, say, at least a few layers and a handful of input variables, such a mathematical problem is not remotely tractable due to the deep and non-linear nature of neural networks. Thus, only approximated solutions are in order. To compute one, we can resort to iterative gradient ascent, backpropagating up the network to the inputs, leveraging the the network's mapping function $f$ being differentiable. One single step of the gradient ascent process goes like this:

$$
\mathbf{x}^{(t+1)} \gets \mathbf{x}^{(t)} + \alpha \nabla_{\mathbf{x}} (s \circ f)(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})
$$

## The trials and tribulations of computing prototypes

### The problem with raw gradients

Naively deriving a solution to the above mathematical problem using the above update rule implicitely assumes that the model's gradients driving the walk in its input space are sufficiently well-behaved to stabilize around images intelligible by humans. However, neural networks usually fall short of supporting that hypothesis.

Conventional discriminative vision-based neural networks are _not_ designed to derive images back from the latent space or preserve an invertible encoding of the original inputs[^note-highly-specific-reversible-nn].

[^note-similarity-transformers-hash]: The crucial difference is that Transformers seek to preserve and reorganize information so that it is stable to local noise in the continuous variables, whereas cryptographic hashes irreversibly destroy evidence of similarity and reshuffle discrete inputs.

Rather, they are optimized to aggregate visual cues and compress information from the inputs into progressively more abstract latent representations of densely packed arrays of numbers that are supposed to be more or less invariant to semantic-preserving changes in the input space. These latents are useful for downstream prediction tasks. Forefront convolutional kernels in CNNs and Transformers patch embedding kernels usually look like Gabor filters or random noise patches [@alexnet2012; @dosovitskiy2021vit].

To me, legacy convolutional models aggregation strategy resembles an elaborate interferometry system detecting edges and alignments of visual patterns [@zeiler2014visualizingCNNs], whereas the Transformer-based models aggregation strategy consisting in gradually refining the residual stream latents is more reminiscent of internal state buffers in cryptographic hash algorithms such as AES[^note-similarity-transformers-hash].

[^note-highly-specific-reversible-nn]: Except highly-specific architectures designed from the ground-up to also be able to possibly process the data in reverse, like Variational Auto-Encoders.

Anyway, as a matter of fact, vision models tend to backpropagate very noisy gradients in the input space, making them almost useless for reconstructing natural images. The presence itself of high-frequency noise in individual filters is not a proof that vision models invariably favor noisy images--because even high frequency patterns can arguably cancel out into lower frequency results--but at least it strongly hints at it[^note-not-llm]. Since CNN and ViT alike seem to rely mostly on locally jittered patterns to capture the semantics of images, nothing really prevents activation maximization from falling into equally jittered local optima, completely overriding the prototype buffer with unnatural high frequency noise. In the field of computer vision, these images are referred to as _adversarial images_.

[^note-not-llm]: Despite the intentional dashes, this sentence is not from an LLM. I wrote this myself, like this whole post, by the way.

This poses a serious problem to gradient ascent approaches, because iteratively updating the pixels of a randomly initialized source image leads to high-frequency visual gibberish that completely ignores the reality of images taken from real-world physical scenes a.k.a. _natural images_.

### Regularizing in model input space

Fortunately, all is not lost. Numerous Pre-2020 research results tackle prototype generation for convolutional networks. I will not reiterate here the already plentiful literature reviews of the various strategies they came up with to circumvent adversarial images. Both @Nguyen2019UnderstandingVisualizing and @olah2017featureVisualization provide one. But let's only state that they all involve some form of regularization in the model input space. Most of them aim to curb parasitic high frequencies, with various implementations techniques (for example directly in the gradients, or in the buffers; or indirectly via intermediary image transforms interleaved with gradient steps).

## Recent advancements in computer vision \& feature visualization

In the course of a global acceleration in machine learning research, the advent of Transformers has completely overhauled the field of computer vision the same way NLP was revolutionized by Transformers a few years prior, coming from cumbersome recurrent neural networks. 

Since the publication of the the first articles about feature visualization on Distill, ViTs have largely taken over convolutional models for vision tasks. Convolutional neural networks have now somewhat fallen into disfavor, and for a good reason: Transformers are more versatile, perform better and and allow to combine tokens of virtually any modality, text and images alike. This major architectural modernization--from ResNet [@2016resnet], to ConvNext [@2023convnext] to ViT [@dosovitskiy2021vit]--unlocked drastically better generalization power between models from the mid-2010's and models from the mid-2020's.

My impression is that computer vision as a domain now elicits far less curiosity about the models' inner working principles and interpretability than it used to do when convolutions were the norm, and it has gotten dragged by fast-paced developments driven by a skyrocketing interest in the field of natural language processing.

I think an indicative example of that trend is the renowned [Transformer Circuits Thread](https://transformer-circuits.pub/), which has shifted from computer vision to NLP since its inception. It seems that vision models have now become a commodity taken for granted, that gets incorporated in all major new image-text model released out there, while the new _El Dorado_ of language processing took control of the AI race monopolizing all the attention[^pun-attention].

[^pun-attention]: Get it? ;\)

The fact remains that over the course of the last five years or so, the quality of image latent representations in frontier vision models has still improved a lot due to scaling up both the neural networks and the datasets in use, and improved training methods. Transitioning from supervised learning to almost exclusively self-supervisied learning certainly has a lot to do with improvements in state-of-the-art models indeed. A very nice review of the iterative research about transcending supervision and leveraging better data augmentation techniques in computer vision can be found on [Lil'log](https://lilianweng.github.io/) [@weng2021contrastive; @weng2019selfsup]. I find that it's a very informative overview of the main contrastive learning techniques that heralded novel, implicit, abstracted-away learning paradigms such as DINO's teacher-student system for robust image representations. Nonetheless, not many groundbreaking achievements have happened since the late-2010's developments when it comes to reverse engineering vision models.

There _is_ a series of recent experiments and papers that are worth seeing, though.
